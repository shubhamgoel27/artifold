Yosemite ValleyTen classic climbs, drawn to scale

You’ve ticked 0 of 10

Ten lines up the Valley, from a 5‑pitch morning to 3,500 feet of El Cap.

Climb these in order and the walls get taller, the nights get longer, and the history gets better. Each one is here because a guidebook, a legend, or a few thousand tick lists put it there.

01

Nutcracker

Manure Pile Buttress

Grade
5.8
Commitment
Grade II
Length
500 ft
Pitches

First ascent
Royal & Liz Robbins, 1967

The Valley’s first multi-pitch, for almost everyone.

Five pitches of every Yosemite crack type in miniature: fingers, hands, a flake, a slab, and a mantel at the very top that has humbled people who just led everything below it. The Robbinses climbed it on nuts alone, an argument against the piton hammer that the whole Valley eventually accepted.

Beta Ten minutes from the road. Go early: by 9 a.m. there is a line.

Snake Dike

Half Dome, southwest face

Grade
5.7 R
Commitment
Grade III
Length
1,000 ft
Pitches
9
First ascent
Eric Beck, Jim Bridwell, Chris Fredericks, 1965

The easiest roped way to the top of Half Dome.

You follow a single dike of rock up a smooth granite apron, where the R in the grade means long runouts on easy rock (a few bolts were added low down in 2025). The climbing is never hard. The approach is the real test, then the summit slabs, then the walk down the cables among day hikers in sneakers.

Beta Budget a long day: the hike in and out is longer than the climb.

Grade III
Length
2,000 ft

First ascent
Kenneth Adam, Morgan Harris, Kenneth Davis, 1936

Sixteen pitches of moderate rock, plus one swing.

Climbed in 1936, decades before El Cap, and still the long route most people do first. Ledges, chimneys and slabs wander up beside the great arches, and halfway there’s a pendulum: hang on the rope and run across the slab, or free it at 5.10.

Beta The descent is long and has its own accidents; go down in daylight.

Washington Column, east face

1,000 ft

First ascent
Warren Harding, Glen Denny, Chuck Pratt, 1959 (aid)
First free

The best long free climb in America. Ask anyone.

Three Stonemasters took Harding’s aid line and freed every pitch in 1975, which reset the bar for what a free climb could be. It’s steep, sustained, and never easy. Most stories are about the Harding Slot, a squeeze chimney that is easier for the thin.

Beta Enduro Corner, a long 5.11 crack, comes early. If it feels desperate, rappel.

05

Steck-Salathé

Sentinel Rock, north face

Grade
5.10b
Commitment
Grade V
Length
1,500 ft
Pitches
15
First ascent
Allen Steck & John Salathé, 1950

The Valley’s great chimney, and its oldest sandbag.

Steck and Salathé spent five days on the face in the summer of 1950, short on water the whole way. Climbers now do it in a day, but it’s still dark, wide and physical, and the grade feels light. Near the top the Narrows pinches down to a body-width slot you wriggle through.

Beta Pack small: anything on your harness will snag in the Narrows.

Multi-day. You sleep on a ledge or a portaledge, and you haul.

First ascent

First free

Steep, clean aid, and the classic first hard wall.

Charlie Porter soloed it alone in 1972 up the steepest part of El Cap’s right side. It overhangs almost the whole way, so dropped gear falls clear and you live in space. Most parties now climb it hammerless, with cams and hooks.

Beta Haul bags hang free here. Practice hauling first.

Regular Northwest Face

Half Dome

Length
2,200 ft

First ascent
Royal Robbins, Mike Sherrick, Jerry Gallwas, 1957

America’s first Grade VI, and it still looks it.

Robbins, Sherrick and Gallwas took five days in 1957 to climb the face Ansel Adams photographed. It was the first route of its size in the country. The famous moment comes high up: Thank God Ledge, a sloping shelf you shuffle along, or crawl, two thousand feet up.

Beta Bivy at Big Sandy if you go in two days. Water is the heavy part.

08

Dawn Wall

El Capitan, southeast face

Grade
5.14d
Commitment
Grade VI
Length
2,500 ft
Pitches
32
First ascent
Warren Harding & Dean Caldwell, 1970 (aid, as Wall of Early Morning Light)
First free
Tommy Caldwell & Kevin Jorgeson, 2015

The hardest big-wall free climb ever done.

Caldwell worked it for seven years. In January 2015 he and Jorgeson lived on the wall for 19 days, and Jorgeson fell on the traverse pitch again and again until he sent it. Adam Ondra repeated it in 2016; Seb Berthe made it three in 2025. You probably won’t climb it, but you can watch it from El Cap Meadow.

Beta Bring binoculars and a folding chair to the meadow.

09

The Nose

El Capitan, south buttress

Grade
5.9 C2
Commitment
Grade VI
Length
3,000 ft
Pitches
31
First ascent
Warren Harding, Wayne Merry, George Whitmore, 1958
First free
Lynn Hill, 1993

The most famous climb on earth goes up the middle.

Harding’s team took 47 days spread over 16 months, using fixed ropes and siege tactics, and topped out at dawn on November 12, 1958. In 1993 Lynn Hill freed it, then did it free in under a day the next year. Every big-wall climber eventually queues here.

Beta Fall is prime season and the crowds come too. A weekday start helps.

Salathé Wall

El Capitan, southwest face

Grade
5.9 C2
Commitment
Grade VI
Length
3,500 ft
Pitches
35
First ascent
Royal Robbins, Tom Frost, Chuck Pratt, 1961
First free
Todd Skinner & Paul Piana, 1988

Plenty of people call it the best climb in the world.

The second route up El Cap, done in 1961 with fixed ropes for only the first third. The Headwall near the top is a steep, airy crack. The Freerider variation is the line Alex Honnold climbed ropeless in 2017.

Beta Haul light to Heart Ledge, then commit. Retreat above it is hard.

Start on Nutcracker. Everyone does, and everyone remembers the mantel.
