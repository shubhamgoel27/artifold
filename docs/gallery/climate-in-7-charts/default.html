Climate change, explained in 7 charts

From the air we breathe to the ice at the poles, the evidence points the same way. Here is the story of a warming planet, told through the data.

1. CO₂2. Temperature3. Sea ice 4. Sea level5. Disasters6. Emissions7. Carbon budget
Chart 1 · The cause

CO₂ in the atmosphere is the highest in at least 800,000 years

~280 ppmpre-industrial level
~427 ppm2025 annual average
+50%increase since 1750

Measurements at Hawaii's Mauna Loa Observatory began in 1958. The curve has climbed every single year since, and the pace is accelerating: about 0.8 ppm per year in the 1960s, nearly 2.5 ppm per year in the last decade. Ice cores show CO₂ never exceeded about 300 ppm in the previous 800,000 years.

Source: NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory, Mauna Loa annual means. 2025 value approximate.
Chart 2 · The effect

The planet has warmed about 1.3°C, and the last ten years were the ten hottest on record

2024hottest year on record
~1.5°Cabove pre-industrial in 2024, a first
0.2°Cwarming per decade, recent rate

Year-to-year swings come from natural cycles like El Niño, but the long-term trend is unmistakable. Each bar shows how much warmer or cooler a year was than the 1951 to 1980 average. Blue years have nearly disappeared.

Source: NASA GISTEMP v4, global land-ocean temperature index. 2025 value approximate.
Chart 3 · The ice

Arctic summer sea ice has shrunk by about 40% since satellites started watching

7.1M km²September 1979
3.4M km²record low, September 2012
~12%loss per decade

The Arctic is warming nearly four times faster than the global average. Each September, at the end of the melt season, sea ice reaches its annual minimum. The area lost since 1979 is larger than India. Less white ice means more dark ocean absorbing sunlight, which drives further warming.

Source: NSIDC Sea Ice Index, September average extent. 2025 value approximate.
Chart 4 · The oceans

Seas are rising, and the rate has more than doubled in 30 years

~11 cmrise since 1993
2.1 → 4.5mm per year, 1993 vs 2024
~21 cmrise since 1900

Two things push sea level up: water expands as it warms, and melting glaciers and ice sheets in Greenland and Antarctica add water to the ocean. Satellite altimeters have tracked the global average since 1993. Even a few centimeters makes storm surges and high-tide flooding much worse for coastal cities.

Source: NASA / CNES satellite altimetry, global mean sea level relative to 1993. Values smoothed and approximate.
Chart 5 · The impact

Billion-dollar disasters in the US have become seven times more frequent

3.3 / yr1980s average
23 / yr2020 to 2024 average
28record set in 2023

Heat waves, floods, wildfires, droughts and severe storms that each cause at least $1 billion in damage (adjusted for inflation). Part of the rise reflects more people and property in harm's way, but a warmer atmosphere holds more moisture and energy, making many extremes more intense and more likely.

Source: NOAA NCEI Billion-Dollar Weather and Climate Disasters (CPI-adjusted), annual average events per decade. NOAA retired this dataset in 2025.
Chart 6 · The driver

Fossil fuel emissions keep climbing, with only one real dip in decades

~38 Gtfossil CO₂ in 2024, a record
6×more than in 1950
-5%2020 pandemic drop, quickly erased

Burning coal, oil and gas releases carbon that took millions of years to bury. Global emissions grew fastest during the post-war boom and again in the 2000s as China industrialized. Growth has slowed in the last decade, and emissions are falling in Europe and the US, but the global total has not yet peaked.

Source: Global Carbon Project, Global Carbon Budget 2024. Fossil fuel and cement CO₂, excluding land use change.
Chart 7 · The deadline

At today's pace, the carbon budget for 1.5°C runs out in about three years

~130 Gtleft for 1.5°C (50% chance)
~42 Gtemitted per year, all sources
~25 yrsuntil the 2°C budget is gone

Warming tracks the total amount of CO₂ ever emitted. That means there is a finite "budget" we can still release to stay under a given temperature limit. Humanity has already emitted roughly 2,600 billion tonnes since 1850. The remaining budgets are small by comparison. Every year of delay spends a big piece of what is left.

Source: Forster et al. (2025), Indicators of Global Climate Change 2024; Global Carbon Project. Budgets from start of 2025, 50% likelihood, rounded.

The bottom line: CO₂ is rising because we burn fossil fuels. That heats the planet, melts ice, raises seas and fuels extremes. The physics also points to the fix: warming stops roughly when net CO₂ emissions reach zero.
