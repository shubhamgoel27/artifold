Five of these seven charts go up. The two that fall are the ice and the time left.

Carbon dioxide, heat, the sea and the bill for disasters all keep rising. At the current rate, the carbon budget for holding warming to 1.5 °C is spent in a little over three years.

Each stripe above is one year of global temperature, after Ed Hawkins. Every panel below is tinted from a later year’s stripe, so the page warms as you read.

Carbon dioxide

For 800,000 years, CO₂ stayed under 300 ppm. Since 1959 alone it has risen by a third.

427ppm

2025 average at Mauna Loa, up from about 278 before industry

Air bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice keep a record of the atmosphere going back 800,000 years. Its highest point was 298.6 ppm. The air passed 400 in 2015, and in 2024 it rose 3.33 ppm, the biggest one-year jump since annual records began in 1959.

Temperature

2024 was the first calendar year above 1.5 °C.

For a century the bars barely move. Then, from about 1980, every decade beats the last. Last year (2025) came in second or third warmest, at 1.44 °C on the WMO’s count, and 2026 has averaged 1.47 °C through August. One year over 1.5 is not yet a breach of the Paris limit, which is measured over 20 years. The world is getting close.

Arctic sea ice

The Arctic’s summer ice now covers about a third less sea than it did in the 1980s.

−36%

Last ten Septembers against the first ten on record

September is when the ice is smallest. The record low came in 2012, and no year since has climbed back to the 1980s average. Less white ice means more dark water soaking up the sun.

Sea level

The ocean is rising twice as fast as it was in 1993.

+10.5cm

Global average since 1993, measured from space

The rate has gone from 2.0 to 4.4 mm a year, as warm water expands and land ice melts into it. Tide gauges put the rise from 1900 to 2018 at about 21 cm.

Closer to home. San Francisco’s tide gauge, one of the oldest in the Americas, has been running since 1854. Since 1897 the bay has climbed 1.98 mm a year, about 25 cm in all.

Disasters

The US now has a billion-dollar disaster every two to three weeks.

19a year

2015–2024 average, up from 3.3 in the 1980s

Storms, floods, fires, droughts and freezes costing at least $1 billion each, after inflation. The count adjusts for inflation but not for growth: there are more people and buildings in harm’s way than in 1980. 2023 set the record with 28.

NOAA stopped updating this count in May 2025. Climate Central now keeps it with the same method and the same lead scientist, and counted 23 for 2025 (the dashed bar).

Emissions

Emissions haven’t peaked. 2025 was expected to set another record.

38.1Gt CO₂

Fossil CO₂ projected for 2025, up 1.0% on 2024

That is four times the 1960 level. China emits more than the US, India and the EU combined, though the average American still emits far more than the average person in China.

Carbon budget

At today’s rate, the budget for 1.5 °C runs out in early 2029.

130Gt CO₂ left

A carbon budget is how much more CO₂ the world can release for an even chance of staying under a given level of warming. Counted from January 2026, 1.5 °C leaves 130 Gt. The world emits about 42 Gt a year.

Every stripe on this page is already drawn, except one.

19962026, undrawn

2026 has run at 1.47 °C through August, the Copernicus service reports that El Niño is strengthening, and its colour isn’t final until December.
