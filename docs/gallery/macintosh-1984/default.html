Origins

Raskin's appliance

The Macintosh project began in 1979 as the brainchild of Jef Raskin, an Apple employee and human-interface thinker who imagined an inexpensive, self-contained "information appliance" that anyone could take out of the box and use. He named it after his favorite apple variety, the McIntosh, deliberately misspelled to sidestep a trademark held by the audio company McIntosh Laboratory.

Xerox PARC and the graphical turn

In December 1979, Steve Jobs and a group of Apple engineers toured Xerox's Palo Alto Research Center, where they saw the Alto: a machine with a bitmapped display, overlapping windows, and a mouse. Apple's Lisa project absorbed those ideas first. The Lisa shipped in January 1983 at $9,995, impressive but far too expensive to sell widely.

Jobs takes over

After being pushed off the Lisa team, Jobs took control of the small Macintosh group in early 1981. Raskin left Apple the following year. Under Jobs, the Mac shifted from Raskin's text-centered, 6809-based appliance to a Motorola 68000 machine with a full graphical interface, effectively a "Lisa for the rest of us." The team worked out of a building on Bandley Drive that flew a pirate flag, embodying Jobs's maxim: "It's better to be a pirate than to join the Navy."

"Real artists ship."Steve Jobs, to the Macintosh team as deadlines pressed in early 1984

Jobs insisted that the inside be as carefully considered as the outside. The signatures of the team members were molded into the inside of every case, visible only to anyone who cracked it open with a long-handled Torx driver.

The "1984" ad

Chiat/Day creative director Clow and director Scott made the commercial that launched the machine.

1984.mov

"On January 24th, Apple Computer will introduce Macintosh. And you'll see why 1984 won't be like '1984.'"

The Macintosh was announced to the world by a single television commercial that aired nationally once, during the third quarter of Super Bowl XVIII on January 22, 1984. Directed by Ridley Scott, fresh off Blade Runner, and created by the agency Chiat/Day (art director Brent Thomas, copywriter Steve Hayden, creative director Lee Clow), it showed a lone athlete, played by Anya Major, sprinting through a gray Orwellian hall and hurling a sledgehammer into a giant screen of Big Brother.

Apple's board reportedly hated the ad and asked Chiat/Day to sell back the airtime. The agency quietly sold only part of it, and the full 60-second spot ran anyway. It became one of the most celebrated commercials in history and helped turn the Super Bowl itself into an advertising event.

The unveiling

Two days later, at the shareholder meeting in Cupertino, Jobs recited a Bob Dylan verse, showed the ad, then walked to a small table and pulled the Macintosh out of a canvas bag. With "Chariots of Fire" playing, the screen filled with demos, and then the machine spoke for itself through text-to-speech: "Hello, I'm Macintosh. It sure is great to get out of that bag." The audience gave a prolonged standing ovation.

Timeline

  1. 1979Jef Raskin starts the Macintosh project inside Apple. In December, Jobs and engineers visit Xerox PARC.
  2. 1981Steve Jobs takes over the Macintosh team; the design pivots to the Motorola 68000 and a full graphical interface.
  3. January 1983Apple Lisa launches at $9,995, previewing the GUI ideas the Mac will bring to a wider market.
  4. January 22, 1984The "1984" commercial airs during Super Bowl XVIII.
  5. January 24, 1984Macintosh is introduced at the Flint Center and goes on sale at $2,495.
  6. Spring 1984Sales start strongly, with roughly 70,000 units sold in the first 100 days, before slowing later in the year.
  7. September 1984Macintosh 512K, the "Fat Mac," quadruples memory; the original is renamed Macintosh 128K.
  8. 1985The LaserWriter printer and Aldus PageMaker arrive, launching the desktop publishing revolution. In September, Jobs leaves Apple after a boardroom struggle with John Sculley.
  9. January 1986Macintosh Plus brings 1 MB RAM, an 800 KB drive, and SCSI, finally making the Mac a capable workhorse.
  10. 1997 onwardJobs returns to Apple; the Mac line continues to this day, a direct descendant of the 1984 machine.

Cultural Impact

Commercially, the original Mac was a mixed result. Its small memory, lack of a hard drive, and closed design frustrated power users, and sales fell well short of Apple's projections after a strong launch. Its influence, however, far outstripped its sales figures.

The GUI goes mainstream

The Mac was the first commercially successful personal computer with a mouse-driven graphical interface. Windows, icons, menus, and the pointer became the default way people would expect to use computers, and Microsoft Windows followed in its wake.

Desktop publishing

Paired with the LaserWriter, PostScript, and PageMaker, the Mac let individuals produce typeset-quality documents, transforming graphic design, printing, and small-press publishing almost overnight.

Typography for everyone

Proportional, multiple fonts on screen and a "what you see is what you get" approach introduced millions of people to typefaces, an idea Jobs later traced to a calligraphy class he took at Reed College.

Design as a product value

The friendly, face-like case, Susan Kare's warm icons, and the smiling startup Mac made computers feel approachable. This attention to industrial design became central to Apple's identity.

Advertising as event

"1984" redefined what a product launch could be and turned Super Bowl commercials into cultural spectacles in their own right.

The "rest of us"

Apple's tagline, "The computer for the rest of us," framed computing as a tool for creative and everyday people rather than specialists, a framing that still shapes the industry.

"The Macintosh was the first computer you could actually fall in love with."A sentiment widely echoed by early users and the press in 1984

Today the original Macintosh sits in the permanent collections of museums including the Smithsonian and the Computer History Museum, and its pixel-perfect icons and Chicago typeface remain shorthand for the dawn of friendly computing.
