Introduced January 24, 1984

This beige box set the pattern for every computer you’ve used since.

It had 128 KB of memory, a 9‑inch black-and-white screen and one floppy drive, and it cost $2,495. By the specs it was underpowered and overpriced. It was also the first computer most people saw with windows, icons, menus and a mouse, and after it, nobody went back.

Notice, as you go, how much of today’s computer was already sitting on that 9‑inch screen.

Notice what isn’t there: no fan, no slots, no command line.

Everything sits in one molded case. You couldn’t open it without a long-shafted Torx driver, and that was the point.

Inside the case

Turn it around: the team signed the inside of the case.

At a signing party on February 10, 1982, the Mac team put their names on a sheet that was molded into the back of every case, about 48 names in all. Here are twelve of the people behind the machine, set in type rather than traced, with what each of them did.

  • Jef RaskinStarted the project in 1979 and named it after his favorite apple, the McIntosh.
  • Steve JobsTook the project over in early 1981 and drove it to launch.
  • Burrell SmithDesigned the digital board, the computer itself.
  • Andy HertzfeldWrote much of the system software and the User Interface Toolbox.
  • Bill AtkinsonBuilt QuickDraw, the graphics engine, and wrote MacPaint.
  • Susan KareDrew the icons and the bitmap fonts, Chicago and Geneva among them.
  • Bud TribbleManaged the software team.
  • Steve CappsCo-wrote the Finder. Also sewed the pirate flag.
  • Bruce HornCo-wrote the Finder.
  • Randy WiggintonWrote MacWrite.
  • Jerry ManockDesigned the case with Terry Oyama, and chose its beige.
  • Joanna HoffmanMarketing, from the team’s earliest days.

Steve Jobs, at the team’s January 1983 retreat in Carmel. That August a pirate flag went up over the Bandley 3 building; Steve Capps sewed it and Susan Kare painted the skull.

After hello

The ad aired nationally once. The idea never stopped airing.

The first Mac sold about 70,000 in its first 100 days, and then demand slowed: 128 KB was too little, and there was almost no software yet. What lasted was the way of working it introduced.

  • The pointer became the default. Windows, icons, menus and a mouse went from a $9,995 Lisa feature to the way a computer was used. Microsoft shipped Windows 1.0 in November 1985.
  • Type became something anyone could choose. Kare’s fonts put real typefaces on a screen. With the LaserWriter and Aldus PageMaker in 1985, the Mac started desktop publishing.
  • A computer launch became an event. Ridley Scott’s “1984,” made by Chiat/Day, set the template for the product launch as cultural moment.
